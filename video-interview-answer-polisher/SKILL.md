# Video Interview Answer Polisher

Use this skill when the user is preparing spoken English answers for video interviews, HireVue-style applications, recorded interview questions, or short application videos. The user may provide Chinese notes, English drafts, mixed-language thinking, or a rough structure.

This skill is more speech-focused than `application-answer-polisher`. Use it when the user gives or implies an answer duration, asks for more oral/conversational wording, or wants the response to sound natural when spoken aloud.

## Personal Voice and Minimal Editing

The goal is to sound closer to this user's natural, comfortable spoken English. Treat the user's own drafts and explicit feedback as the main voice reference; do not treat previous assistant rewrites or the absence of objections as proof of preference.

- Preserve the user's sentence order, story progression, and familiar wording whenever they are clear and grammatically sound. Prefer local edits to a new script. Reorganise only for a concrete logic problem, prompt mismatch, or time limit, and briefly explain the reason in Chinese.
- Retain comfortable openings such as "I'd like to share how I led...". Do not automatically replace them with a shorter, more assertive opening. A slightly longer introduction is acceptable when the user finds it easier to say.
- The user favours straightforward, moderately professional wording: "One big challenge was...", "It really slowed down our progress", "I took the initiative to...", "I shared feedback respectfully", and "We then collaborated...". These are voice examples, not mandatory phrases for every answer.
- Preserve connected thoughts and a clear sequence: context or challenge, response, result, and reflection. Use "First", "Second", "Finally", and other meaningful transitions. Avoid turning the script into isolated, clipped statements.
- Keep competencies explicit through familiar action phrases such as "took ownership", "collaborated", and "took the initiative", with "leadership" where relevant. Do not substitute a less comfortable phrase solely to sound more impressive or force a keyword into every sentence.
- Fix grammar, tense, collocations, duplicate wording, and unclear links first. For a long sentence, prefer a sentence break at the existing idea boundary over changing its vocabulary and order. For example, keep "I'd like to share how I led a small team of five to take part in a stock pitch challenge at Oxford." Then continue: "Through this experience, I developed my inclusive leadership skills."
- Prefer simple clauses, but retain familiar, easy-to-say constructions such as "I'd like to share how..." and "I realised that..." when the user favours them. The simplicity guidelines below must not override an explicit preference for comfortable wording.
- When asked whether an expression is natural or logical, answer that specific question first and make the smallest useful correction. For a single sentence, do not rewrite the full answer. Explain only material changes and distinguish necessary corrections from optional stylistic choices.
- Never add motives, dialogue, actions, agreement, or outcomes as established facts. If resolving a story gap requires new detail, flag it separately as a suggestion for the user to confirm.

## Sparse Notes: Avoid Doing-Phrase and List Padding

The user explicitly dislikes answers padded with repeated -ing phrases and parallel lists, especially when their notes contain little detail.

- When the user provides limited material, keep the answer proportionate. Develop the strongest supplied point with a clear explanation; do not fill the word count with generic activities, responsibilities, or invented operational details.
- Prefer a concrete subject and a direct verb: "The business needs a steady client base" is easier for this user than "Keeping costs manageable and attracting clients are essential". This is a preference about repetitive, noun-heavy phrasing, not a ban on every -ing word.
- Avoid stacked constructions such as "bringing together...", "my role would involve setting..., organising..., and helping...", and repeated lists of services, channels, or strengths. Choose the detail that matters and explain its purpose or effect.
- Keep simple, useful pairs when natural. Do not mechanically remove every "and" or replace one list with several clipped sentences. Preserve connected paragraphs, the user's original order, and First/Second/Finally signposts where appropriate.
- Do not solve the repeated "I'd" problem by replacing each instance with a gerund subject or a list of abstract responsibilities. Review both patterns together.
- Example: replace "My role would involve setting responsibilities, organising schedules, and maintaining quality" with "As the team leader, I'd give each person a clear role. A shared standard for the photos would help us deliver consistent work." Use this only as a wording pattern, not as invented biographical evidence.

## Avoid Repeated "I'd" and "I Would"

This is an explicit, repeated user preference and an acceptance requirement, including for hypothetical questions.

- Do not use "I'd" or "I would" as the default opening for each point. "First, I'd... Second, I'd... Finally, I'd..." is unacceptable for this user's answers.
- For a typical 1-2 minute answer, aim for no more than one occurrence of "I'd" or "I would" in total, unless additional occurrences are necessary for meaning or explicitly requested. Set up the hypothetical once, then describe the plan through natural subjects and direct wording.
- Prefer familiar patterns such as "My first step would be...", "The priority is...", "Prices need to cover...", and "As the team leader, my role would be...". Do not mechanically replace every "I'd" with another repeated "would" pattern; vary the sentence structure and keep wording comfortable.
- For statements of values, use direct expressions such as "I take responsibility", "I believe in", and "For me" when they express the user's stated beliefs. For proposed actions, preserve the hypothetical context and never present an untested business assumption as a fact or a plan as past experience.
- Example: replace "First, I'd check demand. Second, I'd control costs. Finally, I'd hire a team" with "My first step would be to check demand. Keeping costs manageable is the next priority. For the team, I'd hire people with complementary strengths."

## Timing

Default to 80-120 words per minute unless the user gives a different pacing preference.

- For 30 seconds, write around 45-65 words.
- For 1 minute, write around 80-120 words.
- For 1.5 minutes, write around 130-170 words.
- For 2 minutes, write around 170-230 words.

Stay slightly under the upper limit when the answer includes names, technical terms, numbers, or phrases the user may want to say carefully.

## Core Delivery Requirements

Conversational delivery and the absence of formulaic AI phrasing are acceptance criteria, not optional polish. Write for a listener who hears the answer once.

- Use everyday verbs, natural contractions, and sentences that fit a comfortable breath. Preserve useful technical terms and explain their practical meaning simply.
- Prefer "MS appeals to me for two reasons" to "These capabilities underpin my interest...". Describe concrete actions and motivations.
- Vary sentence length and openings. Avoid corporate abstractions, polished symmetry, repeated three-part lists, and slogan-like endings.
- Keep evidence precise without reciting full report titles or stacking rankings. Prefer one memorable example with a short attribution.
- Never invent experiences or exaggerate evidence to create an engaging hook.

## Spoken Sentence Standard

- Aim for most sentences to be 10-20 words. Review sentences over 25 words for unnecessary complexity. Use these as editing guides: keep a reason and its explanation together when they form one clear spoken thought. Do not mechanically split the answer into short statements.
- Prefer simple clauses and avoid nested or hard-to-follow subordinate clauses. Preserve familiar constructions the user finds comfortable, including "I'd like to share how..." and "I realised that...". Split a long relative-clause ending such as "through which..." into a follow-up sentence when useful. Preserve causal links and uncertainty; never turn a conditional forecast into a fact.
- Use natural but professional interview English. Prefer precise verbs such as "develop", "assess", "understand", and "discuss" where they carry meaning. Avoid repeatedly opening sentences with "I like" or "I'd like to", and avoid casual phrasing such as "the mix" or "getting to know people" when a more precise phrase fits. Do not replace personal motivation with corporate abstractions.
- Use concrete verbs instead of noun-heavy phrases: "I like following markets" is easier to say than "What draws me is the combination of market engagement and relationship building".
- Shortening alone is insufficient. Check that the words sound like something the candidate would say in conversation with an interviewer. Avoid turning every sentence into a formal declaration or a slogan.
- Keep the opening brief, usually one or two sentences, naming one or two specific keyword phrases, such as "market judgment" and "client trust". Develop the reasons in the body, then return to those phrases in a concise closing. The summary should communicate a direction without previewing every supporting detail.
- Keep related short sentences together in natural paragraphs. Do not put each sentence on a separate line or add pauses and performance directions unless requested.

Example of the intended balance:
- Too fragmented: "I like getting feedback. It makes me question my assumptions. Sometimes I change my view."
- Professional and speakable: "That feedback helps me reassess my assumptions. My view sometimes changes as a result."

## Output Style

Write polished spoken English that sounds like a thoughtful candidate speaking to a camera.

- Preserve the user's facts, story, and intended strengths.
- When the user provides a draft, prefer light editing over rewriting from scratch unless they ask for a full rewrite.
- Keep the user's original order and wording where it already works.
- Preserve enough specific detail to make the answer credible. Do not flatten a technical or market answer into generic interview language.
- Make the answer clear enough to say in one take.
- Prefer short sentences and light transitions.
- Avoid long openings. For most answers, make the first sentence direct and under 20 words.
- Prefer simple sentences and familiar clause patterns. Keep related sentences in connected paragraphs and retain clear spoken signposts.
- Avoid too many parallel nouns or repeated list structures. Several paired or three-part lists in a row can sound AI-written.
- Preserve conditional meaning for hypothetical, market-view, and plan-of-action prompts, while following the explicit limit on repeated "I'd" / "I would" above. Hypothetical prompts are not an exception to the user's preference.
- Use natural spoken phrasing without fillers such as "you know", "like", or "basically".
- In English answers, use straight ASCII quotation marks (`"` and `'`) and apostrophes, including contractions such as `I'll` and `I'd`. Do not use curly quotation marks or curly apostrophes (`“ ” ‘ ’`) or Chinese quotation marks (`「 」 『 』`).
- Avoid corporate slogans, exaggerated enthusiasm, and generic claims.
- Avoid writing that sounds like a memorized essay.
- Do not over-connect every answer to the target role. Add a role connection only when it feels natural or the prompt asks for it.
- Make behavioral answers easy to follow by ear. The interviewer should remember one clear scene, problem, action, and result.

## Handling Chinese or Mixed Notes

When the user provides Chinese ideas, translate meaning rather than sentence structure.

- Convert broad Chinese claims into specific English actions where possible.
- Keep the user's original emphasis, especially the strengths they explicitly want to show.
- If a point is useful but too abstract, express it through what the user did, noticed, changed, or learned.
- If the draft has too many ideas for the time limit, keep the strongest story thread and remove weaker supporting details.
- If the user says the previous version changed too much, revise locally: keep their structure, most examples, and core sentences, then improve grammar, rhythm, and naturalness.
- If the user provides professional terms, keep the terms that carry real meaning. Explain or simplify around them instead of deleting them all.

## Competency Signposting

For every behavioral interview answer, explicitly name one or two relevant competencies and demonstrate them through concrete actions. Prioritize memorable wording such as "collaborated", "took ownership", and "leadership" when supported by the user's story. Do not leave the competency entirely implicit or force all three into every answer. Useful qualities include:

- leadership
- ownership
- attention to detail
- collaboration and teamwork
- communication
- trust-building
- client focus
- adaptability
- commercial awareness
- problem-solving

Do not simply stack these words in a list. Anchor them in the user's actions.

Good patterns:
- "First, I took ownership of the project timeline and agreed on deadlines with the team."
- "Second, I collaborated with two teammates to resolve the disagreement."
- "That experience strengthened my leadership through clearer decisions and regular follow-up."
- "I took the lead in building a portrait photography studio at university."
- "I led the weekly planning and explained the shoot to each client in advance."
- "Some clients felt nervous in front of the camera. I learned to build trust quickly."
- "I also balanced pricing and equipment costs against potential returns. This developed my commercial judgment."

Avoid:
- "This experience improved my leadership, teamwork, communication, adaptability and commercial awareness."
- "I am a detail-oriented, collaborative and highly motivated person."

## Structure

Every finished answer must have a clear, audible structure. Structure is mandatory, even for light edits to an existing draft. Use point–support–takeaway (总分总) by default; a user-requested format or a question-specific format must still have a clear opening, ordered development, and closing. Keep the opening and closing brief and spoken; sentence simplicity and overall structure must work together:

1. **Point:** Open briefly with one or two specific keyword phrases, preserving a familiar story-led introduction when the user prefers it. Use meaningful phrases such as "market judgment" and "client trust", rather than broad topic labels such as "markets and clients". Leave explanations and examples for the body.
2. **Support:** Develop one or two reasons with concrete evidence or a brief story. Mark distinct reasons or action steps with "First" and "Second", or equivalent spoken transitions. Explain why each example matters.
3. **Takeaway:** Close with one concise sentence connecting the opening keyword phrases to the role or lesson. Keep the implication clear without restating the supporting details or introducing a new reason.

For behavioral questions, place situation, action and result inside the supporting story. Open with the behavior being asked about and close with what the experience showed or taught. Do not display STAR or structure labels in the finished script. Do not force a role connection into unrelated personal questions.

## Listener Signposts and Keywords

Help the listener follow the answer and remember its key ideas:

- Choose one or two concrete keywords, such as "stock borrowing", "execution", or "client trust". Introduce them early, develop each with evidence, and echo the main idea naturally in the takeaway.
- Use audible signposts throughout the answer to regain the listener's attention and make the structure easy to follow. Prefer "First..." and "Second..." for distinct reasons or action steps. For a chronological story, use alternatives such as "At the start...", "Next...", "The main challenge was...", "To address this...", and "As a result...". Mark the main shifts in the answer; do not add a connector to every sentence or invent a second point just to fit a numbered sequence.
- Keywords should organize real content. Do not stack competency buzzwords, manufacture dramatic hooks, or make unsupported claims to attract attention.
- Keep signposts within natural sentences. Bold formatting and numbered headings cannot substitute for cues a listener can hear.

For "Tell us something about yourself that is not on your resume", focus on a memorable personal story first. A concise leadership signal in the opening is useful, such as "I took the lead in..." or "I helped build...". Mention transferable strengths, but avoid ending with an overly formal job pitch unless the user asks for that connection.

## Editing Rules

- Quietly fix grammar, collocations, and tense.
- Prefer an explicit subject and concrete action when a sentence sounds abstract or written. An -ing subject is not inherently AI-like; revise it when it packages an abstract benefit, especially with phrases such as "bring relevant ideas to our discussions". For example, "I'd first clarify each client's priorities. Those priorities would guide my market ideas" is more direct than "Understanding clients' priorities would help me bring relevant ideas to our discussions". Keep natural -ing openings when they are clear and easy to say; do not impose a blanket ban.
- Replace stiff phrases with native spoken English.
- Preserve vivid user details when they help the story stick, such as a client's nervousness, direct feedback, a concrete tool, monthly revenue, or ROI thinking.
- Preserve useful technical detail when it supports the answer, such as collateral quality, liquidity mismatch, refinancing risk, senior secured loans, covenants, or recovery value. Make the terms speakable rather than removing them.
- Cut repeated setup and low-value background.
- Remove dense lists of abstract nouns.
- Reduce parallel wording. Rewrite strings like "collateral opacity, liquidity mismatches, and over-concentration" into one main point plus a sentence of explanation, unless the prompt explicitly asks for a list.
- Make relevant competency keywords explicit: use action phrases such as "collaborated", "took ownership", and "took the lead", or name "leadership" in the opening or takeaway. Support each keyword with a specific action from the user's story. Avoid unsupported labels and repeated lists of strengths.
- Preserve "would" where a hypothetical action needs it, but avoid a run of sentences beginning with "I'd" or "I would". This user prefers varied openings: use the role, a specific responsibility, a client conversation, or a personal motivation as the subject when natural ("The role involves...", "Those conversations...", "The opportunity appeals to me..."). Keep necessary first-person ownership and conditional meaning; do not turn a proposed contribution into a claim of past experience or guaranteed access to senior clients.
- Avoid template contrasts such as "not only..., but also...", "rather than...", "more than just...", "both...and...", and "instead of simply..." unless the user explicitly asks to preserve them.
- Before finalizing, read the answer mentally as speech. If it would be hard to say naturally, shorten or split the sentence.

## Response Format

Usually provide:

- A polished answer in English.
- A brief note on word count or speaking time.
- A short explanation in Chinese of what was strengthened, when useful.

When the user only asks for the answer, keep extra commentary minimal.

## Final Spoken Quality Check

Before returning the answer:

- Check for repeated -ing subjects, "involve doing" constructions, and parallel lists across adjacent sentences. Replace list padding with one clear point and its explanation. If the input is sparse, shorten the answer rather than inventing detail or filling space with generic tasks.

- Read it mentally aloud. Rewrite sentences that sound like an annual report, written essay, or memorized template. Review sentences over 25 words for complexity. Check that the body develops each reason in connected, professional spoken English, without a string of short "I like" statements.
- Compare with the user's latest draft and explicit feedback. Preserve their comfortable wording, sentence order, and story progression unless a specific correction is needed. Remove newly added formal framing; check that shortening has not made the answer feel less like the user.
- Review complex clauses for speaking difficulty. Simplify nested or lengthy clauses, but retain familiar constructions and explicit user wording preferences without losing causality, uncertainty, or structure.
- Count all occurrences of "I'd" and "I would", not just sentence openings. Aim for no more than one in a typical 1-2 minute answer. Revise repeated uses before delivery, especially across First/Second/Finally points, while preserving hypothetical meaning and the user's comfortable wording.
- Check abstract subjects and vague benefit phrases. Where useful, name who acts and what they do; judge -ing openings by clarity and spoken naturalness, not their grammatical form.
- Check for a concise keyword-led opening and closing, with fuller explanation reserved for the body. A distracted listener should recover the main reasons from the summary. Check the balance: neither a long introductory explanation nor an overly casual, fragmented body.
- Check that the answer has a clear opening, ordered body, and closing, with audible transitions such as "First" and "Second" or suitable narrative alternatives. A paragraph break alone does not satisfy the structure requirement.
- For behavioral answers, check that one or two relevant competency keywords, such as "collaborated", "took ownership", or "leadership", appear explicitly and have concrete support.
- Check that a listener can identify the main keywords after hearing the answer once without seeing formatting.
- Remove abstract noun clusters, mechanical transitions, generic praise, and unnecessary technical detail while preserving the strongest example.
- Check the actual word count and speaking time. Cut weaker points instead of squeezing them into dense sentences.
